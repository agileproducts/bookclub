# Thinking in Systems

## The Basics

_A system is an interconnected set of elements that is coherently organised in a way that achieves something_

_A system is more than the sum of its parts_

Systems can consist of tangible and intangible parts. Culture is intangible.

How do you know if you are looking at a system?
* Can you identify parts?
* Do the parts affect each other?
* Do the parts together produce an effect that is different from the effect of each part on its own?
* Does the effect, the behaviour over time, persist in a variety of circumstances?

_Many of the interconnections in systems operate through the flow of information_

A system's function or purpose is not necessarily made explicit (the goal!).

_An important function of almost every system is to ensure its own perpetuation_

Systems can be nested within systems.

You can imagine the relative importance of a system's elements by imagining them changed one by one.

If the elements change, a system generall carries on being itself. If the interconnections change, it may be greatly altered. Changes in function or purpose can also have drastic effect.

The parts of the system we are most likely to notice - the elements - are often the least important part.

The foundation of any system is the *stock* of elements. 

_A stock is the memory of the history of changing flows within the system_.

If you understand the dynamics of stocks and flows you understand a lot about the behaviour of complex systems.

As long as the sum of inflows exceeds the sum of outflows, the level of stock will rise, and vice-versa. If the flows are equal the system will be held in dynamic equilibrium. 

It's quicker and easier to change a flow than affect the stock. A stock takes time to change bcause flows take time to flow. Stocks have intertia and can act as buffers.

_Stocks allow inflows and outflows to be decoupled and to be independent and temporarily out of balance with each other_.

Systems thinkers see the world as a collection of stocks as along with the mechanisms for regulating the stocks by manipulating flows.

If you see a behaviour that persists over time there is likely a control mechanism creating that consistent behaviour, and that mechanism operates through a *feedback loop*.

_A feedback loop is a closed chain of causal connections from a stock, through a set of decisions or rules or physical laws that are dependent on the level of the stock, and back again_.

Balancing feedback loops seek stability. Reinforcing feedback loops run away.

The size it takes for an exponentially growing stock to double in size equals approximately 70 divided by the growth rate expressed as a percentage.

## The Systems Zoo

## One stock systems

### A stock with two competing balancing loops - e.g. a thermostat. 

_The information delivered by a feedback loop can only affect future behaviour, it can't correct instantly_ - There is always a delay in response, systems have inertia.

_A stock maintaining feedback loop must have its goal set to compensate for drains or losses._ Otherwise it will miss target. The temperature will undershoot desired.

### A stock with one reinforcing loop and one balancing loop - e.g. a population

_Complex behaviours of systems often arise as the relative strength of feedback loops shift causing first one then the other to dominate behaviour_

If the reinforcing loop always dominates you get exponential growth, if the balancer always dominates it will go to zero. In situations like economic predictions it's important to think about how the driving forces may change over time. Systems analysis models what would happen if the driving factors evolve in different ways.

Questions for value-testing a model:

1. Are the driving factors likely to evolve this way?
2. If they did, would the systems react this way?
3. What is driving the driving factors?

_Systems with similar feedback structures produce similar dynamic behaviours_

### A system with delays - e.g. business inventory

This is like the thermostat but behavious oscillates because of the lag. 

_A delay in a balancing feedback loop makes a system likely to oscillate_

Our perceptions often make us react in a way that makes the oscillation worse. 

_Changing the length of a delay may make a large change in the behaviour of a system_

Seeing the delays is crucial to systems thinking.

## Two stock systems

_A renewable stock constrained by a nonrenewable stock - e.g. an oil economy_

Growth subject to an external constraint like natural resources.

_In physical exponentially growing systems there must be at least one reinforcing loop and at least one balancing loop, because no physical system can grow forever in a finite environment_

The balancer may not yet have become strong , but it will.

Like oil becoming more expensive to extract the more you extract the easy bits.

_A quantity growing exponentially towards a constraint or limit reaches that limit in a surprisingly short time_

### A renewable stock constrained by a renewable stock - e.g. a fishing economy

_nonrenewable resources are stock limited_

_renewable resources are flow limited_

Renewable resources can support harvest or extraction indefinitely, but only at a finite flow rate equal to their regeneration rate. If they are extracted faster than they regenerate they may eventually be driven below a critical threshold and become for all practical purposes, nonrenewable.

Three possible behaviours:
* Overshoot and adjustment to a sustainable equilibrium
* Overshoot beyond that equilibrium followed by oscillation around it
* Overshoot followed by the collapse of the resource and the industry dependent on it

Which outcome occurs depends on the critical threshold beyond which the ability of the resource to renew itself is damaged, and on the rapidity and effectiveness of the balancing feedback loop that slows the rate of depletion as depletion occurs. If the feeback is fast enough equilibrium is reached before the threshold is crossed.

## Systems and Us

### Resilience

Resilience is a measure of a system's ability to survive and persist within a variable environment.

Resilience arises from multiple feedback loops that restore a system after peturbation.

If there are feedback loops that restore the feedback loops the system is even more resilient.

Feedback loops that can adapt or evolve are even better. Systems that can do that are _self organising_.

_There are always limits to resilience_

Systems which are very static over time can lack resilience. 

Systems need to be managed over time not just for productivity or stability, but also resilience.

### Self-organisation

The capacity of a system to make its own structure more complex is called 'self-organisation'.

### Hierarchy

A self organising system often generates a hierarchy of subsystems.

Complex systems can evolve from simple systems only if there are stable intermediate forms. The resulting complex forms will naturally be hierarchic.

Hierarchies reduce the amount of information that any part of the system has to keep track of.

In hierarchical systems relationships within each subsystem are denser and stronger than relationships between subsystems. In well functioning systems feedback delays are minimised and no level is overwhelmed with information.

Hierarchies evolve from the lowest level up. The original purpose is always to help the originating subsystems do their job better.

When a subsystem's goals dominate at the expense of the total system's goals the resulting behaviour is called **suboptimisation**.

## Why systems surprise us

1. Everything we think we know about the world is a model.
2. Our models are usually pretty good.
3. But they don't represent the world fully.

## Beguiling events

Events can seem random, but the behaviour of a system reveals itself over time and is the result of the underlying system structure.

News is events. Econometric models are behaviour based, but tend to overemphasise flows over stocks. But flows are driven by stocks, not each other.

We are too easily distracted by events.

## Linear minds in a nonlinear world

In a linear rekationship one element is proportional to the other. In a nonlinear one it is not.

It is hard for our minds to predict nonlinear relationships.

## Nonexistent boundaries

We imagine boundaries that aren't there. A 'side effect' is simply an effect of the system. It's part of it, not outside it.

But in any model, you need to draw a boundary somewhere or all problems are the universe. We have to invent them for clarity and sanity.

Systems analysts often fall into the trap of undersimplifying and producing vast unparseable diagrams - hello Miro. Enormously complicated analyses produce piles of information that only serve to obscure the answers to the problems at hand.

_Boundaries are of our own making and they can and should be reconsidered for each new discusion, problem or purpose_

## Layers of limits

_At any given time, the input that is most important to a system is the one that is most limiting_

But the limiting factor can change as the system evolves. Growth itself depletes or enhances limits.

Any physical system with multiple inputs and outputs is surrounded by layers of limits. 

There will always be limits to growth. If they aren't self-imposed they will be system imposed.

## Ubiquitous delays

Delays are ubquitous. Every stock is a delay. Most flows have delays.

Delays are often the sensitive leverage points for changing the behaviour of a system.

Where there are long delays in feedback loops, some sort of foresight is essential.

## Bounded rationality

Bounded rationality means that people make quite reasonable decisions based on the information that they have, but they don't have perfect information.

We are _satisficiers_ rather than _optimisers_. We attempt to satisfy our needs based on what we know, but we rarely find the long term optimum. 

We have cognitive biases and fallacies that lead us to misperceive risk, misweight signals, over-discount the future etc.

Change comes from getting an overview outside of the limited information seen. from any single place in the system. From a wider perspective information flows, incentives etc can be restructured so that more optimal results can be achieved.

## System traps and opportunities

The world is nonlinear and can't be made otherwise for administrative convenience. All systems surprise us.

But some systems are _perverse_. They are structured in a way that produces problematic behaviour.

Archetypes for these kind of systems are _addiction_, _drift to low performance_, _escalation_. These are system traps. But since they can be fixed, they are also opportunities.

### Policy resistance

Policy resistance comes from the bounded rationality of each actor in the system, each with their own goals. The subsystems have different goals, and act as feedback loops that resist change for the overall system. 

You can try to overcome policy resistance with sheer power and this can work, but it is dangerous.

Or you can just let go and give up ineffective policies, spend the energy elsewhere. This may provide an opportunity to better understand the design of the system. This is often the best solution.

The most effective solution is to align the goals of the subsystems, usually by providing an overarching goal.

### The tragedy of the Commons

In a commons system there is a shared erodable resource that each user of the resource is incentivised to increase their use of at a rate which is not influenced by the condition of the commons.

The same can happen with common sinks, like dumping waste.

Ways to avoid this are:

* Education and exhortation - help people understand the consequences
* Privatise the commons - make each person responsible for their own share of the resource and feel the consequences
* Regulate the commons - quotas, taxes, incentives

### Drift to low performance

Some systems don't just resist change, they keep getting worse.

In a system like this the actor in a feedback loop has a goal or desired system state and takes action to reduce the discrepancy between that and the actual system state. But in this system there is a discrepancy between the actual system state and the perceived one, the perception is worse than the reality, and the desired state is influenced by the perception. The result is a strong reinforcing feedback loop downhill. The lower the perceived state, the lower the desired state, the less correcting action is taken, the lower the system state..

A system with eroding goals of this type is often referred to as being like boiling a frog.

There are two antidotes:
* Keep standards absolute
* Make goals sensitive to the best past performance and not the worst


### Escalation

Escalation comes from a reinforcing feedback loop set up by competing actors trying to get ahead of each other.

Each actor takes their desired state from the other's perceived system state.

Ways out:
* Unilateral - reduce your own system state to induce the other actor to follow
* Multilateral - negotiate

### Competitive Exclusion

Where successful actors are rewarded in a way that helps them become even more relatively succesful, a reinforcing feedback loop is formed which creates winners and losers.

Ways to escape this are:
  * Diversification - find a way to break the competitive trap by exploting different resources
  * Regulation - provide loops that prevent winners taking all, e,g. competition policy
  * Equalisation - have mechanisms to redistribute resources
  
### Addiction

In a system which creates addiction or dependence, there is a stock with in and outflows. It could be physical or meta, like a sense of self-worth. The actor maintains the stock by adjusting a balancing feedback loop, altering the in or out flows. The actor has a goal and compares it to their perception. The argument in the book here doesn't really hang together.

In this system, the burden to correct the system falls on an external intervener which improves the perception but weakens the ability of the system to support itself.

To fix this, interventions need to be designed in a way that helps the system to shoulder its own burdens, rather than taking it over in a liberal intervention.

* If you are the intervenor, work in such a way as to restore or enhance the system's ability to support itself
* If you are the intervenee, build up your own capabilities before removing the intervention

### Rule beating

Rule beating is evasive action to get around the intent of a system. Obeying the letter of the law, but not the spirit. It becomes a problem if it leads to large distortions. 

Rule beating usually indicates a problem with the design of the system. Trying to stamp it out through greater enforcement usually leads deeper into the trap. The way out is to understand rule beating as a feedback mechanism to improve the design of the system.

### Seeking the wrong goal

One of the most powerful ways to influence the behaviour of a system is through the purpose or goal. If the goal is badly designed, the results won't be as desired.

The way out is to specify indicatiors and goals that reflect the real welfare of the system.

## Leverage Points - Places to Intervene in a System

Leverage points are places in the system where a small change could lead to a large shift in behaviour.

People involved in a system often know intuitively where the leverage points are but push in the wrong direction.

Stocks that are big relative to their flows are more stable, they have large buffers. You can make a system more stable by increasing buffers but if they are too big the system becomes hard to change. And buffers can have costs - inventory.

The only way to fix a system that is laid out poorly is to rebuild it.

Delays in feedback processes are points of high leverage but are often not easy to change.

The strength of balancing feedback loops can be a leverage point. These are important relative to the strength of the effect they are designed to correct. If the effect increases in strength, we must increase the strength of the feedback loop.

Reinforcing feedback loops can be strong leverage points. use these before reinforcing ones.

Information flows.

Self-organising.

Paradigms are the mindset from which systems arise. If they can be changed the leverage is very high. Systems modellers try to do that by building a model of the whole system and being able to see it from the outside.

The ultimate leverage is to transcend paradigms.
